# The Unsolved Archive — Kuchisake-onna


Final editing script · 11:00 · 40 scenes · English


## Scene 01 · 00:00–00:16

THE FEAR WAS REAL


Japan. Nineteen seventy-nine. Adults patrol the streets, searching for a woman described by terrified children. She wears a mask. She asks one question. But here is the contradiction... the fear was real. The supernatural suspect was never verified.


Evidence label: ILLUSTRATED RECONSTRUCTION


## Scene 02 · 00:16–00:33

REPORTED RUMOR · LATE 1978


One influential origin account begins in rural Gifu, late nineteen seventy-eight. An elderly woman was said to have seen a figure with a mouth stretched toward both ears. The location varies between accounts. Even the original witness story remains unconfirmed.


Evidence label: REPORTED RUMOR · RECONSTRUCTION


## Scene 03 · 00:33–00:50

A PUBLISHED RUMOR


A newspaper reference gives us a firmer anchor. A National Diet Library entry identifies a January twenty-sixth, nineteen seventy-nine item in a Gifu newspaper. That establishes a published reference to the rumor. It does not establish the existence of the woman herself.


Evidence label: LIBRARY REFERENCE · PUBLICATION ≠ PARANORMAL PROOF


## Scene 04 · 00:50–01:07

A DESCRIPTION THAT CHANGES


Her description changed with every telling. Tall. Long dark hair. Sometimes a beige coat, sometimes red. A white surgical mask. Some versions gave her a height of one hundred seventy-five centimeters. None of these measurements identifies a verified individual.


Evidence label: CONFLICTING RUMOR DESCRIPTIONS · NOT AN IDENTIFIED PERSON


## Scene 05 · 01:07–01:19

AM I PRETTY?


The central encounter was brutally simple. A woman stops a child and asks... ‘Am I pretty?’ If the answer is yes, she removes her mask. Beneath it lies a mouth split almost to the ears. Then comes another question.


Evidence label: FOLKLORE ENCOUNTER · NO VERIFIED ATTACK


## Scene 06 · 01:19–01:31

EVEN LIKE THIS?


‘Even like this?’ In the story, neither ordinary answer guarantees safety. Say no... and she may attack. Say yes... and she may try to give you her face. The question itself becomes the weapon.


Evidence label: LEGEND VERSIONS DIFFER


## Scene 07 · 01:31–01:47

A STORY CROSSES JAPAN


By spring, the story was traveling beyond Gifu. Children carried it between schools. Families repeated it over telephone lines. Newspapers and magazines amplified it. Within months, versions circulated across Japan... from northern communities to the far south.


Evidence label: ILLUSTRATIVE RUMOR NETWORK · NOT VERIFIED SIGHTINGS


## Scene 08 · 01:47–02:05

THE WALK HOME


The response moved beyond playground gossip. Schools and families organized escorted journeys home. Adults conducted patrols. Some accounts describe increased police presence. Precautions were being taken against a figure whose identity and alleged supernatural powers could not be confirmed.


Evidence label: ILLUSTRATED RECONSTRUCTION


## Scene 09 · 02:05–02:17

BUILT BY RETELLING


As the tale expanded, so did the monster. Some children said she carried scissors. Others described a sickle. Some claimed she could outrun anyone. Each new detail made the woman more frightening... and harder to disprove.


Evidence label: CONTRADICTORY DETAILS WITHIN THE LEGEND


## Scene 10 · 02:17–02:30

A DIFFERENT ORIGIN STORY


But where did her mutilated face come from? Modern retellings offer a much older answer... a samurai, a betrayal, and a punishment carved into flesh. The story is unforgettable. Its historical foundation is another matter.


Evidence label: A LATER-ASSOCIATED ORIGIN LEGEND


## Scene 11 · 02:30–02:48

A FEUDAL HOUSEHOLD


One popular explanation takes us back to feudal Japan. A beautiful woman lives within a powerful samurai household. Some versions call her his wife. Others, his concubine. The details change... but each version begins with beauty treated as something a man can own.


Evidence label: FOLKLORE DRAMATIZATION


## Scene 12 · 02:48–03:03

BETRAYAL — IN THE LEGEND


The tale says jealousy consumed the samurai. Perhaps he suspected an affair. Perhaps the accusation was enough. What matters is the punishment he supposedly chose. He would not simply reject the woman. He would destroy the feature by which others admired her.


Evidence label: UNVERIFIED LEGEND · SYMBOLIC RECONSTRUCTION


## Scene 13 · 03:03–03:19

A PORTRAIT TORN IN TWO


In the most brutal retelling, the blade tears across her face. Her mouth becomes a wound extending toward the ears. The samurai then demands an impossible answer: who could ever call her beautiful now? This is the legend's central act of cruelty.


Evidence label: SYMBOLIC PAPER PORTRAIT · NO REALISTIC INJURY


## Scene 14 · 03:19–03:35

WHO CONTROLS THE FACE?


Now consider what the story is really doing. It turns violence into a question about appearance. The wounded woman is denied control over her own face. Her imagined revenge begins with forcing strangers to repeat the judgment that destroyed her.


Evidence label: LITERARY INTERPRETATION


## Scene 15 · 03:35–03:56

ONRYŌ: A LITERARY LENS


Japanese ghost traditions include the onryō... a spirit driven by intense resentment. In this interpretation, Kuchisake-onna fits the pattern. A violent wrong produces a relentless haunting. But that resemblance is literary analysis, not proof that a real murdered woman became the monster of nineteen seventy-nine.


Evidence label: ONRYŌ: FOLKLORE ARCHETYPE · NOT PROOF OF AN ORIGIN


## Scene 16 · 03:56–04:17

NO VERIFIED HISTORICAL LINK


Here the investigation reaches a missing link. No reliably identified samurai, concubine, crime date, or surviving case record establishes this episode as the beginning of the modern legend. We have a compelling explanation for the monster's appearance... but not a demonstrated chain of historical events.


Evidence label: NO ESTABLISHED CHAIN OF HISTORICAL EVENTS


## Scene 17 · 04:17–04:38

A MOTIF IS NOT A FAMILY TREE


Older Japanese ghost literature also contains disturbing images of women with abnormal mouths. Those motifs matter. They show that the visual idea was not entirely new. Yet resemblance alone cannot establish direct descent. The masked woman asking children about beauty is a distinctly modern configuration.


Evidence label: RESEMBLANCE ≠ PROVEN DIRECT DESCENT


## Scene 18 · 04:38–04:56

THREE STORIES. NO PROVEN CAUSE.


Other versions blame failed cosmetic surgery. Some tell of personal betrayal. Others offer no human past at all. Every explanation tries to solve the same mystery: why would a woman's mouth become the center of an endless interrogation? None has been confirmed as the original cause.


Evidence label: COMPETING ORIGIN STORIES · NONE CONFIRMED AS THE FIRST CAUSE


## Scene 19 · 04:56–05:13

AN ORDINARY MASK


The modern surgical mask changes everything. It makes the threat ordinary. A masked passerby would not necessarily seem unusual. Only when she removes it does the familiar become impossible. The legend exploits that tiny interval between recognition... and terror.


Evidence label: THE FAMILIAR BECOMES UNCANNY · SYMBOLIC VISUAL


## Scene 20 · 05:13–05:30

WHY 1979?


The feudal explanation offers a villain, a victim, and a curse. But it cannot explain why the story suddenly overwhelmed Japanese schoolchildren in nineteen seventy-nine. For that, we must leave the samurai behind... and examine the society that carried the rumor.


Evidence label: SHIFTING FROM ORIGIN LEGEND TO SOCIAL CONTEXT


## Scene 21 · 05:30–05:48

THE EVENING JOURNEY


Late-nineteen-seventies Japan was changing. Cities and suburbs were linked by expanding routines of work, commuting, and education. For children, the evening could mean another journey after regular school. The danger they imagined was not inside an ancient castle. It waited along a familiar road.


Evidence label: LATE-1970s SOCIAL SETTING · RECONSTRUCTION


## Scene 22 · 05:48–06:07

KAGI-KKO: SOCIAL CONTEXT


The expression kagi-kko described children carrying house keys and spending time without a parent immediately present. Changing family schedules made childhood independence a subject of concern. It is tempting to blame parental absence for the legend... but that is an interpretation, not a demonstrated universal cause.


Evidence label: POSSIBLE SOCIAL ANXIETY · NOT A PROVEN UNIVERSAL CAUSE


## Scene 23 · 06:07–06:23

JUKU: CONNECTING NEIGHBORHOODS


The stronger explanation lies in the cram school... juku. Students arrived from different neighborhoods. One child described the masked woman. Another carried the story into a second school. Soon, separate communities were exchanging the same frightening image... while adding details of their own.


Evidence label: COMMUNICATION BETWEEN SCHOOL DISTRICTS


## Scene 24 · 06:23–06:44

FEAR AFTER DARK


After lessons, students walked through streets that looked different after dark. An unfamiliar adult, a distant movement, or an ordinary masked stranger could become a frightening possibility. Iikura suggests that anxieties about dangerous strangers were projected onto Kuchisake-onna. The monster gave those uncertainties a visible shape.


Evidence label: PSYCHOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION · NO CONFIRMED STRANGER ATTACK


## Scene 25 · 06:44–07:00

EACH RETELLING ADDS A DETAIL


Rumors mutate because people do not repeat them perfectly. One child mentions a mask. Another adds scissors. A third claims the woman can run faster than any athlete. The changes make the warning easier to remember... and more difficult to distinguish from something actually witnessed.


Evidence label: RUMOR MUTATION · NOT A DOCUMENTED INCIDENT


## Scene 26 · 07:00–07:17

THE FEEDBACK LOOP


The newspapers did not have to invent the apparition to accelerate its spread. Children carried stories into public discussion. Media reports made them visible to new audiences. Those audiences repeated the warnings. A feedback loop formed... with fear functioning as its own distribution system.


Evidence label: MEDIA FEEDBACK LOOP · ILLUSTRATIVE DIAGRAM


## Scene 27 · 07:17–07:36

PRECAUTION IS NOT PROOF


Adults responded because children's fear demanded attention. Patrols and escorted walks made practical sense as precautions. But official concern about safety should not be mistaken for official confirmation of a ghost. A community can respond to a threat report even when the report's central claim remains unverified.


Evidence label: SAFETY PRECAUTIONS ≠ CONFIRMATION OF A GHOST


## Scene 28 · 07:36–07:53

A REPORTED HUMAN IMITATOR


Stories about human imitators circulated too. A secondary account describes an alleged arrest in Himeji in nineteen seventy-nine. The original arrest record has not been established here. Even if that report is accurate, it concerns a human imitator... not proof of the apparition.


Evidence label: SECONDARY ACCOUNT · PRIMARY RECORD NOT ESTABLISHED HERE


## Scene 29 · 07:53–08:10

THE RUMOR LOSES FORCE


The panic did not remain at its peak forever. On June fifteenth, nineteen seventy-nine, a Gifu newspaper described the disturbance as finally subsiding locally. As summer holidays approached, the wider rumor also weakened. Yet declining fear did not erase the character from memory.


Evidence label: LIBRARY-REFERENCED REPORTING · LOCAL DECLINE, NOT A NATIONAL END DATE


## Scene 30 · 08:10–08:30

A SHARED EXPECTATION OF DANGER


This is the phenomenon's most revealing contradiction. No verified supernatural attacker was necessary for the legend to alter behavior. Ordinary objects became frightening. Streets gained new meanings. Parents changed routines. The woman with the torn mouth had become something larger than an alleged sighting... a shared expectation of danger.


Evidence label: SYMBOLIC VISUAL · A SHARED EXPECTATION OF DANGER


## Scene 31 · 08:30–08:43

THE FIRST QUESTION


Return to the encounter. The woman blocks the road. She asks... ‘Am I pretty?’ It sounds like an ordinary question. But within the story, your answer determines what happens next. The conversation has already become a trap.


Evidence label: WITHIN THE LEGEND


## Scene 32 · 08:43–08:55

THE RULES CHANGE


If you say yes, she removes her mask. Her mouth stretches toward her ears. Then comes the second question... ‘Even like this?’ The first answer was never enough. The rules change after you have committed yourself.


Evidence label: FOLKLORE REVEAL · CYAN PAPER SEAM, NOT A REAL WOUND


## Scene 33 · 08:55–09:09

THE YES BRANCH


Say yes again, and in some versions she marks your face to resemble hers. Agreeing does not protect you. Instead, the legend transforms your politeness into permission for the next horror. Even the apparently safe answer becomes dangerous.


Evidence label: ONE VARIANT · FICTIONAL CONSEQUENCES


## Scene 34 · 09:09–09:23

THE NO BRANCH


Say no, and other versions threaten an immediate attack. The story defeats the normal instinct to choose between honesty and agreement. A child confronted with the question is given no reliable answer... and no reliable way to escape.


Evidence label: OTHER VARIANTS · NOT VERIFIED SURVIVAL ADVICE


## Scene 35 · 09:23–09:39

THE SO-SO LOOPHOLE


Some versions offer a loophole. Answer... ‘So-so.’ Neither beautiful nor ugly. The ambiguous reply interrupts the forced choice. This is not verified survival advice. It is a storytelling device that lets the listener imagine escaping an impossible situation.


Evidence label: A STORYTELLING LOOPHOLE · SOME VERSIONS ONLY


## Scene 36 · 09:39–09:55

BEKKŌAME


Another variation gives the child a sweet: bekkōame, traditional amber-colored hard candy. Offering it supposedly distracts the woman long enough to flee. The detail seems absurd... until you understand that children were creating rules that restored a feeling of control.


Evidence label: BEKKŌAME: A DETAIL IN SOME TELLINGS, NOT A PROTECTIVE MEASURE


## Scene 37 · 09:55–10:12

POMADE. POMADE. POMADE.


Then there is the strangest escape: pomade. Say the word three times, some versions insist, and she retreats. Pomade is a hair-styling product, not a chemical spell. The arbitrary rule works because folklore allows its audience to invent weaknesses for an otherwise unbeatable monster.


Evidence label: POMADE IS A HAIR PRODUCT · THE RULE IS FICTIONAL


## Scene 38 · 10:12–10:27

THE RULES WERE NEVER FIXED


Notice the contradiction. The same ghost has different weaknesses in different neighborhoods. The supposedly unbreakable rules were never fixed. They were revised as the story traveled. The monster was assembled collectively... and every storyteller could change the machinery.


Evidence label: THE RULES CHANGE BETWEEN REGIONS AND RETELLINGS


## Scene 39 · 10:27–10:43

A NEW DELIVERY SYSTEM


Decades later, Kuchisake-onna survives in films, games, illustrations, and online stories. The delivery system has changed. The mechanism has not. A frightening image enters a community. People repeat it, reshape it, and give it new reasons to remain.


Evidence label: CONTEMPORARY MEDIA · FICTIONAL POSTS


## Scene 40 · 10:43–11:00

THE FACE WE GAVE TO FEAR


The evidence does not establish a supernatural killer. It establishes something more human... fear, repetition, and collective imagination. In nineteen seventy-nine, Japan gave those forces a face. And behind that face was a question nobody wanted to answer. The Unsolved Archive.


Evidence label: THE EVIDENCE CONCERNS HUMAN FEAR AND RUMOR
